Question Four · Group Behaviour

What is group and its characteristics and type of group?

3
A4 PagesDefinition · types · stages
Page 1 of 3Question 4 · What a group is

Definition of a Group

A group is two or more people who interact with one another, recognise themselves as a unit, and share a common goal or interest. Two strangers waiting at a bus stop are only a crowd; the moment they begin to work for the same result — and see themselves as "we" — they become a group.

In management, a group is the basic unit of work. A computer centre is itself a group of people: a centre head, lab administrators, faculty and support staff, each doing a part of one service. How well that group functions decides how well the centre runs.

Characteristics of a Group

Interaction. Members talk, exchange files, hold meetings and react to each other. Without interaction there is only a collection of individuals.

Common goal. Every group exists for a reason — finish a project, run a lab, prepare for an exam. A shared goal holds the members together.

Perception of membership. Members know who is inside and who is outside, and they say "our team" rather than "that team".

Norms and roles. Groups develop unwritten rules (come on time, share notes) and give each member a part to play — leader, organiser, technical expert.

Cohesiveness and interdependence. The stronger the attraction between members and the more they need each other's work, the stronger the group.

Types of Groups

Groups are classified in several ways, and one group can belong to more than one type — a lab team is formal, task and primary at the same time.

Why the distinction matters. A manager cannot order an informal group into existence, but can influence it. If the informal leaders trust the plan, the formal change spreads much faster; if they resist, even a correct instruction stalls.

Page 3 of 3Question 4 · How groups develop

Stages of Group Development

Tuckman described five stages that a group passes through. They are useful because the leader's job is different at each stage.

Applying it in a computer centre. A new support team first exchanges names and waits for instructions (forming), then argues about who handles which lab (storming). Once duties are written down it settles (norming), and only after that does it actually fix systems quickly (performing). When the project ends, the group disbands (adjourning).

Practical points for a manager: give clear goals early, take conflict seriously but do not fear it, publish the rules the group itself agrees on, and protect the performing stage from unnecessary reorganisation.

Conclusion

A group is two or more interacting people with a shared identity and goal. It may be formal or informal, primary or secondary, command, task, interest or friendship — and it develops through forming, storming, norming, performing and adjourning.

Page 1 of 3Question 5 · What organisational design is

Organizational Design

Organizational design is the process of choosing and arranging the structure of an organisation — its departments, reporting lines, authority and coordination — so that it can achieve its goals. It is the deliberate answer to the question: who does what, who decides, and how does information move?

Design is not the same as a drawing on a wall. It decides where decisions are taken, how many people one manager can guide, and which units must talk to each other every day. A computer centre may be designed as one functional department, or split into teaching, maintenance and networking units.

Elements of Design

Division of labour. Work is broken into tasks so that each person becomes skilled at a small part.

Departmentalisation. Those tasks are grouped by function, product, customer or location.

Chain of command and span of control. Authority runs from top to bottom, and the span decides how many subordinates one manager supervises.

Centralisation or decentralisation. Decisions may sit with the head, or be pushed to the people closest to the work.

Coordination and formalisation. Rules, meetings, schedules and information systems bind the units together.

FunctionalSpeciality (accounts, IT, sales)One product, stable work, deep skill needed

MatrixFunction plus project at the same timeProjects need specialists from many departments

Network / virtualCore staff plus partnersOutsourcing and widely spread talent

Functional design is the usual starting point because it uses specialists efficiently. Its weakness is that departments optimise their own work and forget the customer. Divisional design fixes that by giving each product its own complete team, at the cost of duplicated staff. Matrix keeps both lines open, so an employee may report to a functional head and a project manager; it improves coordination but creates two bosses.

Team-based and network designs are the modern answer to changing demand: a small core coordinates partners, and decisions sit with the team that does the work. They are flexible but depend heavily on communication and trust.

Advantages of Organizational Design

Clarity of work. Each person knows the task, the owner and the deadline, so duplication and gaps disappear.

Efficient use of resources. Specialists are grouped, equipment is shared, and the organisation does not pay twice for the same skill.

Clear authority and accountability. The chain of command shows who may decide and who answers for a failure.

Better coordination. Deliberate links — meetings, reports, a single information system — stop departments from working against each other.

Faster decisions where they matter. Decentralised design lets the person closest to the problem act without waiting for the head office.

Scalability. A well-designed structure can add a new lab or a new course by adding a unit, not by rebuilding the whole organisation.

Motivation and development. Defined roles and career paths give people something to grow into, and teamwork satisfies the social needs described by group theory.

Design choiceAdvantageTrade-off
FunctionalDeep expertise, low duplicationSlow across departments
DivisionalFocus on product or customerDuplicated functions

For a computer centre the practical conclusion is to design around the service it gives: a teaching lab needs scheduling and support roles, a network unit needs clear authority for changes, and an escalation path must be written down so that a failed system reaches the right person quickly.

Conclusion

Organizational design arranges work, authority and coordination so goals can be met. Its advantages are clarity, efficiency, accountability, coordination, faster decisions, scalability and motivation — provided the structure is matched to the work it must do.
