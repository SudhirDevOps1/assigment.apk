Question Four · Devices and Media

Explain multimedia input and output technologies.

3
A4 PagesInput · output · conversion
Page 1 of 3Question 4 · Input technologies

What Multimedia I/O Means

Multimedia combines text, graphics, audio, video and animation in one presentation. Input technologies are the devices and methods that bring those media into the computer; output technologies present them back to the user. A microphone, a scanner and a camera are inputs; a monitor, a printer and a speaker are outputs.

Every media type needs its own capture method. Text is typed on a keyboard, read by an OCR scanner, or decoded from a barcode. Graphics arrive through a flatbed scanner, a digital camera or a graphics tablet. Audio is captured by a microphone or entered as MIDI. Video comes from a webcam, camcorder or capture card. Position and motion come from a mouse, joystick, touchscreen or sensor.

The quality of an input depends on its sampling: a scanner's optical resolution, a microphone's sampling rate, a camera's megapixels and frame rate.

Page 2 of 3Question 4 · Output technologies

Output Technologies

Displays. A monitor may be LCD, LED-backlit LCD or OLED. The important figures are resolution (1920 × 1080), colour depth (24-bit true colour), refresh rate (60–144 Hz) and response time. A projector uses the same idea on a large screen, and an OLED panel can switch pixels off completely for true black.

Printers. Impact printers strike a ribbon: dot-matrix is slow but cheap, and can print carbon copies. Non-impact printers — inkjet, laser and thermal — are quieter and sharper. A laser printer works with a drum, toner and heat; an inkjet sprays droplets. A plotter draws continuous lines and is used for maps and engineering drawings.

Sound and touch. Speakers and headphones convert a digital signal back through a DAC, while haptic devices output vibration or force as another feedback channel.

A monitor is measured in pixels per inch on screen, while a printer is measured in dots per inch on paper. 300 DPI is normal office print; 1200 DPI and above is photo quality.

Page 3 of 3Question 4 · Conversion and comparison

Conversion Between the Two Worlds

An input device performs analogue-to-digital conversion: it samples the signal and rounds each sample to a number (quantisation). An output device performs the reverse. This is why the same photograph looks different on a screen and on paper — the screen adds light in RGB, while the printer subtracts it with CMYK ink, and the two colour ranges do not fully overlap.

Devices are joined by standard interfaces: USB for scanners and keyboards, HDMI and DisplayPort for monitors and projectors, 3.5 mm jack or Bluetooth for audio, and Wi-Fi for streaming. A slow interface becomes the bottleneck when large media files are moved.

PointInput technologyOutput technology
DirectionReal world → computerComputer → real world

Modern devices often do both jobs: a touchscreen is input and output at once, and a smartphone contains camera, microphone, display and speaker in one unit. Interactive multimedia depends on this two-way flow.

Conclusion

Input technologies digitise text, graphics, sound and video; output technologies turn those numbers back into light, ink and sound. The quality of a multimedia system is decided by the weaker end of this chain.

Explain how audio and video are used for multimedia.

3
A4 PagesAudio · video · delivery
Page 1 of 3Question 5 · Audio in multimedia

Audio: Hearing the Message

Audio is the only medium that runs in real time and needs no attention. It is used for narration (teaching a topic), music (setting mood), sound effects (feedback for a click or an error) and speech in video. A presentation without audio feels incomplete because the eye can read only one thing at a time.

Sound is a pressure wave; the computer stores it as numbers. Three settings decide its quality: sampling rate (how many times per second the wave is measured, 44.1 kHz for CD), bit depth (how precisely each sample is stored, 16-bit) and channels (mono, stereo, surround). The Nyquist rule says the rate must be at least twice the highest frequency — 44.1 kHz covers the 20 kHz limit of human hearing.

MIDI is different: it stores instructions ("note C4 on, velocity 90") rather than sound, so files are tiny and can be re-orchestrated. Digital audio (WAV, AIFF) is exact but large; MP3, AAC and OGG shrink it by removing sounds the ear will not notice.

Page 2 of 3Question 5 · Video in multimedia

Video: Motion and Demonstration

Video is a rapid sequence of still pictures. The eye blends them when they arrive at about 24 frames per second or faster; cinema uses 24, television 25 or 30, and games 60 or more. Each frame has a resolution — 1280 × 720 (HD), 1920 × 1080 (Full HD), 3840 × 2160 (4K) — and an aspect ratio, usually 16:9.

Raw video is enormous. One second of Full HD at 25 fps needs about 155 MB before compression, so video is always compressed twice over: spatially inside a frame and temporally between frames. This produces three frame types — I (complete), P (predicted from an earlier frame) and B (predicted from both directions). A group of pictures (GOP) repeats an I-frame so playback can recover.

  I ──── B ──── B ──── P ──── B ──── B ──── P
  │      ↑      ↑      │      ↑      ↑      │
  full   from   from   from   from   from   from
  frame  I & P  I & P  I      P & P  P & P  earlier P
         small  small  medium        (B frames are smallest)
  I = intra (self-contained)   P = predicted   B = bi-directional

The compressed stream is wrapped in a container such as MP4, MKV, AVI or WebM, which also carries audio, subtitles and timing. The codec (H.264, H.265/HEVC, VP9, AV1) does the actual compression. H.265 halves the bit rate of H.264 at the same quality, but needs more processing power.

Page 3 of 3Question 5 · Using audio and video together

Combining Sound and Picture

Synchronisation. Audio and video travel as separate streams inside one container and are joined by timestamps. If they drift apart by more than about 80 ms, the viewer notices lip-sync error, so players drop or repeat a frame to stay aligned.

Streaming. Downloading a whole file before playing is slow. Progressive streaming starts after a buffer; adaptive streaming (HLS, MPEG-DASH) offers several quality levels and switches as bandwidth changes, so the picture keeps playing instead of stopping.

Interaction and access. Audio and video become multimedia rather than plain recordings when the user can pause, seek, caption or annotate them. Captions and transcripts also make the material usable without sound, and a poster frame gives the user something to recognise before playback starts.

Conclusion

Audio carries explanation and emotion; video carries demonstration and motion. Both are sampled, compressed and time-stamped, and a multimedia product succeeds when the two stay in sync and fit the bandwidth of its audience.

Page 1 of 3Question 6 · What the two terms mean

Hypertext and Hypermedia

Hypertext is text that is not read in a single straight line. It is broken into nodes (also called lexias), and the nodes are joined by links that the reader chooses. The idea comes from Vannevar Bush's Memex (1945), and the word itself was coined by Ted Nelson in 1965. The World Wide Web is the largest hypertext system ever built.

Hypermedia is the natural extension of the same idea to all media: a node may contain text, an image, a sound clip, an animation or a video, and a link may open any of them. Nelson used "hypermedia" for this wider system. In short: hypertext links words to words; hypermedia links any medium to any medium.

Page 2 of 3Question 6 · Hypertext in practice

How Hypertext Works

A hypertext document is a graph, not a book. Each node is a page or a section, each link has a source anchor (the words that are clickable) and a destination, and the reader builds a personal trail. In HTML the link is written with the <a href="page.html"> tag, and the anchor text tells the reader what waits on the other side.

Well-written hypertext follows a few rules: a link label should describe its destination ("security levels", not "click here"), every page should offer a way back, and a node should be short enough to read without scrolling far. Navigation aids — a table of contents, breadcrumbs, search and a back button — prevent the famous "lost in hyperspace" problem.

  ┌─────────────────────────────┐
  │  COM / DCOM — Topic 3       │
  │  DCOM extends COM across    │
  │  machines using [RPC]  ──┼──▶ RPC page
  │  See also [security]   ──┼──▶ Security page
  └─────────────────────────────┘
        ▲                    │
        │  back link         └──▶ next topic
  Every underlined phrase is an anchor; the reader decides the order.

Common uses include online help, encyclopedias, e-books, legal documents and code documentation, where a definition must be reachable from every place that mentions it. The strength of hypertext is fast, personal access to related text; its weakness is that a weak structure leaves the reader jumping without learning.

Page 3 of 3Question 6 · Hypermedia and comparison

Hypermedia and the Differences

Hypermedia keeps the same node-and-link structure but allows every node to carry any medium. A history page can open a speech recording, a geography page can open a rotating globe, and a recipe can play a video of the technique. The extra media make the material richer, but they also make it heavier: video needs bandwidth, images need alt text, and audio needs captions.

Point of comparisonHypertextHypermedia
ContentText onlyText, image, audio, video, animation
Link targetAnother piece of textAny media object
BandwidthVery lowHigh — media files dominate
Devices neededAny text displayDisplay, speaker, codec support
ExampleHTML help page, e-bookWebsite with video, e-learning module
RelationThe base ideaHypertext extended to all media

So hypertext is a subset of hypermedia. Every hypermedia system is also hypertext, but a hypertext system need not carry a single picture. In practice the web blurred the line: a modern HTML page usually mixes both, and the term hypermedia is used when the non-text media are essential to understanding rather than decoration.

HypermediaNode.html
<!-- Hypermedia node: links interactive graphics and audio directly -->
<a href="topic_details.html">
  <figure><img src="model.svg" alt="Model"><figcaption>Interactive Diagram</figcaption></figure>
</a>
<audio controls src="narration.mp3"></audio>

Conclusion

Hypertext links text to text and keeps reading fast and light. Hypermedia links every medium to every other and makes the material richer but heavier. The structure is identical — only the range of media changes.
